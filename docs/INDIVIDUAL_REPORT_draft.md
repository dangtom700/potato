## Overview

Our team attempted an ambitious ball launcher: a 12 VDC gearmotor drives a crankshaft that reciprocates a single-acting air cylinder, compressing air into a tank; once a target pressure is reached, a solenoid releases the stored air to push a dart that launches the ball. The launch angle is fixed at 45°, and two ball types are distinguished automatically (by size, via IR beam-break sensors) so the target pressure can be set per ball. My individual contribution was the electronics — a custom motor-drive and interface board — and the control system, developed in MATLAB/Simulink/Stateflow. The concept was elegant on paper but proved too aggressive at the mechanical energy source, and that single assumption ultimately decided our competition result. The following reflects on each subsystem, the competition, teamwork, and our design process, referencing the team's Integration Documents (MATLAB code, circuit diagrams, Simscape model, and photos) as an appendix.

## Mechanics and dynamics

The central weakness was a **load-matching (stall) failure**. As tank pressure rises, the force the crankshaft must exert on the cylinder rises with it, so the torque demanded of the gearmotor climbs steeply near the top of each compression stroke. At the design pressure, the required torque exceeded what the motor and gearbox could deliver at any usable speed; the motor stalled against the back-pressure and, on demo day, drew locked-rotor current until it **burned out**. In hindsight the failure was predictable from a torque–speed vs. pump-load-line comparison, and there were classical remedies we never pursued: a higher gear ratio (trading pumping speed for torque), a smaller-bore cylinder (less force for the same pressure), multi-stroke pumping through a check valve into a reservoir rather than fighting full pressure each stroke, or a pressure-relief limit to cap the load. The crankshaft geometry also concentrated force near top-dead-center, worsening the peak torque demand. This was a teammate's domain, but understanding *why* it failed reshaped how I think about matching an actuator to its load before committing to it.

## Electronics, electrical and interface

This was my strongest and most complete contribution, and it worked. I redesigned the board from the earlier reports to incorporate components that had not been in the original scope — the solenoid driver, the pressure sensor, and the IR ball-detection sensors. The motor is driven by a TB6568KQ H-bridge commanded through SN7400 NAND logic, with the microcontroller issuing only PWM and a direction bit. After diagnosing repeated transient-induced microcontroller failures (a board was destroyed by inductive/brown-out spikes during direction reversals), I hardened the design with bulk and ceramic decoupling, a flyback-clamped and opto-isolated (4N26 + MOSFET) solenoid driver, series protection on the logic lines, and single-point (star) grounding. The pressure transmitter is a 4–20 mA current-loop device, which I interfaced to the 5 V ADC with a 220 Ω sense resistor and an RC filter — a genuinely industrial signal-conditioning problem. The electronics were the one part of the system that performed reliably through the competition; the lessons in power integrity, floating-input hygiene, and sensor interfacing are the ones I will carry forward.

## Control and operation

I designed the control architecture as a Stateflow supervisory state machine (idle → detect → compress → fire → reset) wrapped around a discrete-time PI pressure regulator with anti-windup and integrator-reset gating, triggering the launch on-the-fly the instant measured pressure crosses the target. Target pressure comes from a tailored inversion table (launch distance → release pressure), and because the angle is fixed the controller only needs to command one variable — a deliberate simplification over designs that must resolve angle and acceleration together. I modelled the plant in Simscape, deriving it down from a meter-out reference (3/2 valve, double-acting cylinder, flow control) to a single-valve, single-acting, pressure-driven cylinder that matched our real mechanism, and wrote MATLAB scripts to identify the voltage→pressure dynamics for offline PI tuning. Honestly, though: **the loop was never tuned on hardware**, because system identification and tuning are highly sequential and depend on a functioning compressor that never existed. As a stop-gap I fell back to the position-control module proven in the final control lab, hard-coding encoder positions to approximate a fixed number of pump strokes. So the control system was validated in simulation and in design intent, but its closed-loop performance was never demonstrated on the real plant.

## Competition

On demo day the motor burned out under the back-pressure, and the only remaining way to compress the air and trigger a launch was a **manual hand crank**. Through a favourable interpretation of the rules we were granted three trials: one overshot, one landed in the small scoring square, and one struck the rim. We captured most of our points from the automatic ball-detection task, finishing with **110 points — among the three lowest-scoring teams**. Comparing our approach to others is instructive: our fixed-angle, single-pressure-input design genuinely reduced the control problem (no inverse kinematics, one commanded variable), and in principle offered high energy and pressure-based repeatability. But the teams that scored well used simpler, robust energy sources — springs or servos — that could not stall and did not depend on a difficult motor-to-load match. Our design was theoretically the more controllable, but practically the least reliable, and reliability is what the competition rewarded.

## Teamworking

The most useful teamwork lesson was about **decision-making under expert dissent**. Our mechanical lead, Taurus, warned from the start that a 12 VDC motor was the wrong energy source for a compressor and preferred a pneumatic approach he had more experience with; the team proceeded anyway. When a domain expert flags a core-assumption risk, a team needs a deliberate way to either derisk it quickly or change course — we did neither. Contribution was also uneven: Taurus carried the mechanical build and iterated many reprints, while the detailed-design ownership and CAD workload were not well balanced, which created friction and rework. A silver lining was that the electronics and control work could proceed asynchronously from the mechanical build, given only a few interface specifications (solenoid voltage, pressure-sensor output, IR sensor behaviour) — that clean-interface decoupling is a practice worth repeating. If I were to run it again I would insist on earlier, blunter conversations about the highest-risk assumption and clearer ownership of each subsystem.

## Design processes used by the team

Our process was too **sequential and insufficiently risk-driven**. The single most failure-prone element — whether the motor could actually compress air against back-pressure — was never proven with an early, cheap prototype; instead it was assumed and only discovered at integration. Because the control effort depended on a working compressor for system identification and tuning, the entire back half of the project was gated behind a mechanical build that never succeeded, leaving much of the team idle while one person fought the mechanics. A better process would have front-loaded a feasibility test of the compressor concept, run subsystems concurrently behind defined interfaces (which we did do well on the electronics side), and treated the domain expert's warning as a trigger for a formal risk check rather than an opinion to work around. The project was, above all, a lesson that ambition must be paired with early validation of the assumption everything else rests on.

## Closing reflection

The launcher failed where it was riskiest and succeeded where it was well-engineered. I am proud of a robust, protected board and a coherent control architecture that were let down by an unvalidated mechanical premise. The clearest takeaway is one of process discipline: identify the assumption that carries the whole design, prove it first, and let ambition follow evidence rather than precede it.
